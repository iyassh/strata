\begin{abstract}
We report a negative result and the protocol that produced it. Discovering
Petri nets from fault-free operation of three building HVAC systems, we tested
whether conformance against them detects seeded faults. Over all 73 seeded
scenarios no fault is detected only by the two conformance channels; removing
them changes no scorecard and lowers one system's false-alarm rate from four
days in 96 to one, three of the four being calibration-target rows. Three
further measurements support that reading: a measured circularity bound,
1.78\% against 5.24\% recall at one threshold, obtained by partitioning the
event alphabet across two pipeline versions; a reversal probe showing that two
of the three discovered nets carry no ordering information in their fitness;
and alignment-based support that adds nothing over counting. We report the
protocol --- pre-registration with binding falsifiers, adversarial
recomputation, tests that pin disclosures --- as what made the null checkable.

\keywords{Process mining \and Conformance checking \and Event abstraction \and
Negative results \and Reproducibility \and Cyber-physical systems.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction: an empty quadrant, and what we found in it}
\label{sec:intro}

Process mining has spread from business processes into cyber-physical and sensor-driven domains, and the spread has been reported as a success. One domain of that kind is missing. Building heating, ventilation and air-conditioning (HVAC) operation is a continuous-flow, standard-governed, heavily instrumented process, and it does not appear: the one systematic review of process mining on sensor data whose scope could have captured it records no building HVAC application~\cite{brzychczy2025pmsensorreview}, and a survey of 211 AI-based HVAC fault-detection studies has no process-mining entry at any leaf of its method taxonomy~\cite{bi2024aihvacreview}.

The quadrant looked worth filling for a specific reason. The largest empirical characterisation of HVAC faults, across more than 60{,}000 pieces of equipment in 317 buildings, catalogues the ten most common air-handler faults~\cite{crowe2023faultprevalence}, and on our reading seven of them are behavioural rather than component-level: schedules at the wrong hours, overrides never released, resets skipped, economiser sequences executed out of order. These are deviations in the ordering of operating states, and the threshold alarms and rule libraries buildings actually run see a wrong order only if someone wrote a rule for it in advance. Our hypothesis was the obvious one: a model of the order in which a building moves between states should have an advantage on exactly the faults that dominate the field. We pre-registered it with the observation that would refute it, and built the pipeline to test it.

It was refuted, and this paper reports the refutation with the measurements that explain it. Discovery, conformance and detection were built before any result below was known, and the falsifier for the founding hypothesis --- that a hand-written rule would out-cover the discovered models on the same scenarios --- was written down before the rule existed. That discipline is what turns an unsuccessful application into a reportable measurement: we can say not only that the method did not work but by how much, against what, and on which of its assumptions it failed. The field has almost no published record of that; the one precedent we know of, in another cyber-physical domain, is a preprint reporting that na\"{\i}ve process mining on network event logs was ineffective for intrusion detection~\cite{zhong2022pmids}. Ours is accompanied by artefacts from which every number regenerates. The HVAC domain is the setting; the results are about process mining.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Conformance as an anomaly detector.}
Scoring traces against a discovered model to flag anomalous ones is an established line~\cite{bezerra2013anomalytraces}, on conformance foundations laid by Rozinat and van der Aalst~\cite{rozinat2008conformance,vanderaalst2012replaying,carmona2018conformancechecking}. The line then moved: neural sequence models trained on event logs are reported to outperform conformance-based baselines on standard corpora~\cite{nolle2022binet}. Our null belongs with that migration rather than against it. What the neural line has not done, and Section~\ref{sec:null} attempts, is to hold the comparison fixed at the level of the individual channel inside one detector on one dataset, where the question --- is the conformance signal ever the thing that catches a fault? --- has a yes-or-no answer per scenario that can be read off a committed artefact.

\paragraph{Event abstraction and leakage.}
Turning sensor streams into activity sequences is the binding step for any sensor-domain application; we follow Janssen and colleagues~\cite{janssen2021sensoreventlog} within the taxonomy of van Zelst and colleagues~\cite{vanzelst2021eventabstraction}. The one decision in that step with a measurable consequence is a leakage decision, in the sense Weytjens and De~Weerdt catalogue for process-mining experiments~\cite{weytjens2022dataleakage}: Section~\ref{sec:abstraction} measures what a discovered model loses when it is no longer allowed to recognise the analyst's own rules.

\paragraph{Precision, and why we did not use it.}
Whether a discovered net constrains order or merely permits a set of activities is what precision metrics are meant to answer~\cite{munozgama2010precision,buijs2012quality}; Tax and colleagues catalogue their imprecisions~\cite{tax2018imprecisions}, and vanden Broucke and colleagues show how ill-posed the question is on logs without negative examples~\cite{vandenbroucke2014negativeevents}, which sensor logs never have. We replaced the metric with a direct probe (Section~\ref{sec:discovery}).

\paragraph{Protocol.}
Pitsch and colleagues argue that process-mining research should adopt explicit hypothesis testing~\cite{pitsch2025hypothesistesting}; the pre-registration and binding falsifiers behind this paper are one attempt to practise it. Continuous integration has been used to guard that a published result regenerates~\cite{jimenez2017popper}; Section~\ref{sec:protocol} describes what our tests guard beyond that. Evaluation uses the public, DOI-citable LBNL benchmark family~\cite{granderson2020lbnlfdddata}; the domain's own methods and their reproducibility gap are surveyed elsewhere~\cite{bi2024aihvacreview,mukhtar2025reproducibility}.

\section{Event abstraction, the alphabet wall, and a measured circularity bound}
\label{sec:abstraction}

\subsection{The abstraction}

HVAC operation offers no natural case boundary: a building runs continuously, with concurrent equipment at several granularities and signals that are sampled rather than emitted. We take the calendar day as the case, the shortest unit over which the control sequences of interest --- start-up, occupancy, economiser transitions, night cycling, shutdown --- complete. Streams become events by control-sequence rules grounded in ASHRAE Guideline 36~\cite{janssen2021sensoreventlog}. The canonical alphabet has ten activities in five complementary pairs (system started and stopped, night cycle started and ended, heating active and inactive, cooling active and inactive, economiser window entered and exited);\footnote{\texttt{outputs/grammar\_results.json}, \texttt{canonical\_alphabet} and \texttt{event\_days}.} the single-duct air handler's configuration yields six of them, the two fan-powered units all ten. Because every activity has an inverse that also occurs many times a day, the traces are loop-heavy --- the calibrated healthy band for cooling-active occurrences on the series unit runs from 4 to 44 in a single day --- and the alphabet is constructed, not emitted: each activity exists because a rule decided that a sustained threshold crossing counts as a state change. That is ordinary for sensor-domain process mining~\cite{vanzelst2021eventabstraction}, and it is the opening through which the analyst's judgement enters the model.

\subsection{The alphabet wall}

The events are partitioned into two alphabets that never mix. \emph{State events} are neutral facts about what the equipment did and are the only events admitted to discovery. \emph{Fault-signature events} already encode engineering suspicion --- a valve commanded open while the coil it feeds does nothing, a supply temperature contradicting the unit's claimed mode --- and are withheld from discovery entirely, though they remain available to the detection side. The partition exists because of circularity, the process-mining form of leakage~\cite{weytjens2022dataleakage}: a model discovered over an alphabet that already names faults will later ``detect'' them, but what it has recognised is the analyst's own rule, re-encoded as a transition and read back out through alignment cost. The literature warns about this in the abstract; we are not aware of a prior quantification on a sensor-derived log.

\subsection{The bound}

Quantifying it needs one comparison, and we state exactly what the comparison is. The unpartitioned number comes from the committed artefact of the pre-partition prototype pipeline; the partitioned number from this pipeline's artefact on the same single-duct system. The two share the alignment threshold, the 248-day training and 87-day holdout split, the 4{,}540 scored fault days, and --- the check that the comparison is not confounded elsewhere --- an identical rules-channel output of 3{,}134 true positives; they do not share a code version, and a controlled same-code re-run is future work. The deployed scorecard, two code generations later, gives the same reading: 86 model-channel days of 4{,}891 evaluable fault days, 1.76\%.\footnote{\texttt{outputs/benchmark\_v6\_sdahu.json}, \texttt{model\_days}.} Under that comparison the model channel's apparent recall rises from 1.78\% to 5.24\% --- 81 detected fault days against 238 --- when the discovered net may contain the activities that name the faults.\footnote{Partitioned run: \texttt{outputs/benchmark\_v3.json}; unpartitioned predecessor: \texttt{outputs/benchmark\_v2\_processheal\_v1.json}. Both report threshold $0.333$, \texttt{train\_days} 248, \texttt{holdout\_days} 87, \texttt{pos\_days} 4{,}540; the \texttt{structure} channel's true positives are 81 and 238.} The difference, 3.5 percentage points, is a measured bound on model self-confirmation for this system and this alphabet. The two artefacts also agree on every combined-detector number --- 3{,}152 true positives and 5 false positives in both --- so the partition changed the deployed detector's output by exactly zero days: every model-channel detection the leak had supplied was already caught by the rules channel, which is the redundancy the ablation of Section~\ref{sec:null} later measured directly. It is a bound on one system and on inflated recall only; the design is generic, and a single 3.5-point bound from one building is a data point we would like to see measured elsewhere.

\subsection{Pinning the partition}

A partition enforced by convention decays, so the separation is held by a tested invariant: injecting fault-signature events into a log must not change any day's fitness against a discovered net, and the test fails at build time if a future edit lets signature events reach the discovery path. We recommend the pattern to anyone building a process-mining pipeline over engineered events: the alphabet is where the leakage lives, and a prose statement that two alphabets are separate is not a control.

\section{Discovery at three strata, and the reversal probe}
\label{sec:discovery}

Petri nets are discovered from the state-event log (Table~\ref{tab:logs}) with the inductive miner~\cite{leemans2013inductiveminer,leemans2014inductiveminerinfrequent} in pm4py~\cite{berti2023pm4py}, at three granularities: a \emph{device} stratum modelling one actuator's lifecycle with one case per device per day, a \emph{unit} stratum modelling the air handler's daily cycle, and a cross-system grammar mapping each system's alphabet onto the canonical ten activities so that a net discovered on one building can be replayed against another's log. All discovery uses fault-free data only.

\subsection{The reversal probe}

Whether such a net encodes temporal order, or merely licenses a set of activities in any arrangement, is the question precision metrics answer least reliably on logs with no negative examples~\cite{tax2018imprecisions,vandenbroucke2014negativeevents}, so we use a direct probe: reverse every trace, re-score it against the same net, and report the drop in mean fitness. A net that constrains order cannot fit a reversed log as well as the original; a net whose fitness is a function of the activity multiset will not notice. The three nets separate cleanly.\footnote{\texttt{outputs/grammar\_results.json}, \texttt{reversal\_probe}.} The single-duct net falls from 0.8861 to 0.5526: it encodes order. The series fan-powered net scores 0.9533 forwards and 0.9533 backwards, and the parallel net moves from 0.9021 to 0.9026, five ten-thousandths in the wrong direction. A five-seed trace-shuffle null in the same artefact agrees: observed minus shuffled mean fitness is 0.2225 for the single-duct net against $-0.0007$ and $-0.0001$ for the parallel and series nets, and anti-alignment precision~\cite{vandongen2016antialignments} asks the same question against the model rather than a shuffled log. Two of the three nets carry no ordering information in their fitness; their high absolute fitness states which activities occurred, not when. A fitness of 0.95 is the number one would quote, the probe costs one re-scoring with no negative examples and no reference model, and it told us that two thirds of our model estate was not doing the thing the method is chosen for. Its limit should be stated with it: it tests whether \emph{fitness} responds to order, not whether the net contains order-encoding structure the log never visits, so a null licenses only the operationally relevant claim that this net's fitness is order-insensitive on this log.

\subsection{The net's reading added nothing over counting}

A further measurement belongs here although it was computed for the test in Section~\ref{sec:transfer}. For each system we counted the fault-free occupied days on which a named activity is replayed as a synchronous move against the discovered unit net. On every system the count equalled the number of days on which the activity simply appears in the raw log: 0 against 0, 166 against 166, and 357 against 357.\footnote{\texttt{outputs/discovery\_predicts\_transfer.json}, \texttt{Q}: \texttt{sync\_days} against the raw occupied-day count.} At the unit stratum the alignment added nothing that counting would not have given, exactly. The device stratum, whose net is pooled across each building's four terminal units, disagrees with the unit stratum about which of the two comparable buildings has the better-supported behaviour: supports of $0.455$ and $0.978$ at the unit stratum against $0.437$ and $0.014$ at the device stratum, an inversion. A quantity reproducible by counting on one stratum and reversed on another is not yet a measurement.

\section{The conformance null over 73 seeded scenarios}
\label{sec:null}

Evaluation used three public LBNL datasets~\cite{granderson2020lbnlfdddata} --- a single-duct air-handling unit and parallel and series fan-powered units, 74 seeded fault scenarios of which 73 are scored, one series-unit file being excluded for a rotated calendar, and the single-duct system's 14 being 20 files of which two families of four are byte-identical copies of one run each~\cite{singh2027erratacompanion} --- scored at day level with the scenario as the unit of analysis~\cite{yuill2013fddprotocol,frank2018fddevaluation}. Eight channels score each day: physics rules, temperature-residual bands, \emph{model conformance} and \emph{device conformance} (alignment cost against the discovered unit and device nets --- these two, and only these two, are the process-mining channels), absence of a scheduled activity, statistical bands on activity counts and on reversals, and an advisory seasonal rate channel. Every channel is calibrated on the fault-free year only and may report only what exceeds its own measured noise floor, a binomial against that channel's holdout rate at $p < 10^{-3}$; the conformance channels are not competing against an uncalibrated strawman.

\subsection{The ablation}

Because the committed scorecards record, per scenario, which gated channels were credited with the detection, the ablation is a read rather than a re-run (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). No scenario on any system is detected only by the two conformance channels. They are credited with none of the 14 single-duct or 23 parallel-unit detections and appear in 7 of the 24 series-unit detections, never alone; removing both channels leaves every scorecard unchanged. Nor is removal merely free. On the series unit the two channels appear on all four false-alarm days of the 96-day fault-free holdout, and deleting them eliminates three (the fourth is also flagged by the frequency channel), so that system's false-alarm rate falls from 4 in 96 to 1 in 96 at no detection cost --- with the caveat that the two conformance channels' thresholds are quantile-fit on those same holdout days, so three of the four are calibration-target rows rather than out-of-sample false alarms, and their removal removes an artefact of calibration as much as a measured error.\footnote{\texttt{outputs/union\_fpr\_sfpu.json}: model channel \{2018-11-29\}, device channel \{2018-01-25, 2018-01-30, 2018-12-28\}, frequency channel \{2018-11-29\}; \texttt{union\_all8} 4 days.} The process-mining channels' entire measurable contribution to this detector was three false alarms.

\subsection{The matched-rule control}

A second control separates what discovery contributes at design time from what it contributes at run time. For each channel a domain expert hand-wrote the rule the channel's behaviour implied and scored it on the same scenarios. Two reproduce their channel exactly --- 141 detections against 141 on the series unit and 206 against 206 on the parallel unit, both the frequency channel on the unstable-room-temperature scenario\footnote{\texttt{outputs/matched\_rules\_\{sfpu,pfpu\}.json}: \texttt{mr2} $=$ \texttt{freq} $= 141$ and $206$.} --- which is the useful half of the finding: discovery located an invariant nobody had written down, and once written down the channel was exactly redundant with it. The founding hypothesis had a falsifier attached and it fired: on the series unit a one-line rule, \emph{occupied workday and air-handler heating never active}, covered 192 fault days against the model channel's 135 on the same scenario with zero false alarms on 365 fault-free days,\footnote{\texttt{outputs/matched\_rules\_sfpu.json}, \texttt{SFPU\_VAVDMPRStuck\_80\%}: \texttt{mr1} $=192$, \texttt{model} $=135$, \texttt{healthy\_fp.mr1} $=0$.} and the 135 rests four fifths on a threshold interpolated from a single holdout day (22 to 26 days under the most conservative threshold). Transplanted, the same rule fires on 231 fault-free days on the single-duct system, 124 on the parallel unit and none on the series unit; we first read the 231 as evidence about the models and retract that reading --- the single-duct system has no heating rule and no hot-water valve, so \emph{heating never active} fires on every occupied day for want of a sensor. The only informative contrast is 124 against 0, and whether the models predict it is the subject of Section~\ref{sec:transfer}.

\subsection{Reading the null}

Three readings are available. The conformance channels might be poorly implemented; but they use standard alignment-based replay~\cite{vanderaalst2012replaying,carmona2018conformancechecking} on nets from a standard miner, and they do detect --- they detect no scenario that is not already detected. At day level their unique contribution is 23 days on the single-duct system, 41 on the parallel unit and 111 on the series unit's 29 scored scenarios, concentrated in five series-unit scenarios at 15--17 days each, every one of which some other channel already detects.\footnote{\texttt{outputs/benchmark\_v6\_*.json}, \texttt{model\_unique\_days}.} The domain might be wrong for the method; partly, and the reversal probe says which part, since two of the three nets do not encode order. The third reading is the one the audits converged on: every genuine model-channel detection we inspected was a \emph{count} deviation --- extra heating episodes, collapsed fan cycles, a vanished daily rhythm --- surfacing through alignment cost, and once a frequency channel measured count deviations directly and two-sidedly, the conformance channels had nothing uniquely theirs. Alignment cost was acting as a noisy, one-sided, expensive frequency detector. If that generalises, conformance-as-anomaly-detection will underperform wherever the discovered net is order-permissive, which is wherever the alphabet is loop-heavy --- and sensor-derived alphabets built from complementary pairs that cycle many times a day are loop-heavy almost by construction. We then checked the log rather than the model. Under reheat-coil fouling --- nine of the thirteen misses --- the state-event log is identical to healthy to the minute (same counts, same durations), so no log-based method can detect it; under valve faults it changes in timing and counts, not order. A pre-registered time-infused state model (day-median on-durations, off-gaps and first-on minutes per state pair, same bands and gate) is significant on 11 of 73 scenarios, on none the deployed channels miss, never earlier, and at five extra false-alarm days per fan-powered system; it adds day-level coverage (120 unique days on one scenario) and nothing else.\footnote{\texttt{outputs/x12\_time\_perspective.json}; the artefact records the pre-registration and the one prediction that was too weak to discriminate.}

\section{A pre-registered test that never had power}
\label{sec:transfer}

One claim for the discovered models remained after the null: that they \emph{locate} invariants, so that a rule written from a model's reading of one building holds on another building where that model's support for the invariant is high. Three hand-written rules had been transplanted across the three buildings with their fault-free firings recorded, so a test was pre-registered: the model-derived quantity --- the fraction of fault-free occupied days whose optimal alignment to the discovered unit net contains a synchronous move on the heating activity --- three predictions and three falsifiers were fixed and committed before computation, the scorer was committed before data and then changed once after the model quantity existed (Monte Carlo to exact permutation $p$, a change that cannot alter the verdict but was made without a dated amendment), and the quantity was computed by an agent instructed not to open the rule-firing artefacts. The whole record, with three dated amendments, is in the repository.\footnote{\texttt{docs/plans/2026-09-23-discovery-predicts-transfer-prereg.md}.}

The test failed for four reasons, each caught later than it should have been, and Table~\ref{tab:amend} lists what each amendment recorded and what it got wrong. The undocumented scorer change is the first; the second amendment records it. The seal was also narrower than intended: the pre-registration's own disclosure section printed the transplanted rule's firings, so the two informative cells were unblinded by construction. An amendment made before computation excluded the single-duct system for the correct reason given in Section~\ref{sec:null}, and a second, after the fact, blamed the resulting six-cell design for the test's lack of power, since two groups of three admit a minimum exact permutation $p$ of $3!\,3!/6! = 0.05$ against a criterion of $p < 0.05$. That diagnosis was itself wrong. The model quantity is a property of a building's net and is constant across rules within a building (0.000, 0.455 and 0.978); the nine cells were three buildings pseudo-replicated, the valid permutation null is over buildings, and with three the minimum attainable one-sided $p$ is $1/3! = 0.167$; with the two buildings the amended test actually scored it is $1/2! = 0.5$. The test never had power at any stage. A third amendment records this and voids the one cell the second had kept as informative, whose predictor the first had already disqualified. The reported $p = 0.20$ is not a measurement, and this paper draws no inference about the claim in either direction. What was observed --- the series unit's net treats heating as obligatory (356 of 364 fault-free variants contain it, none fits once it is deleted) while the parallel unit's treats it as optional --- is reported as observation only.

The lesson is the contribution. Pre-registration fixed the quantity, the predictions and the falsifiers; it did not fix the unit of analysis, and two amendments by the same author under the same discipline did not either. A power calculation on the wrong unit survives pre-registration intact. The remedy, recommended to anyone pre-registering a process-mining hypothesis over a handful of systems, is a mandatory field naming the unit of replication and the minimum attainable $p$ under a permutation of that unit, computed and committed before any prediction is written. Had it existed, this test would have been declared unpowered at registration and three buildings reported as what they are: too few.

\section{Tests that pin refutations and disclosures}
\label{sec:protocol}

Every result here is guarded by an automated test that runs on every commit, and what the suite guards is the transferable part. Beyond regenerating results~\cite{jimenez2017popper} and a byte-reproducibility standard on the single-duct system, it pins three things we have not found guarded elsewhere. It pins \emph{fired falsifiers}: the sampling-rate experiment's finding is its four refutations, and a regeneration that lost one fails the build as a lost feature would. It pins \emph{disclosure obligations}: that the false-alarm artefact still declares which channels' holdout rows are calibration targets, that the post-selection defence for a demoted channel ships beside the result it defends, and that the artefact of Section~\ref{sec:transfer} still states its design could not attain its criterion. And it pins \emph{provenance}: a test derives from the git history that the pre-registration, its first amendment and the scorer are ancestors of the commit that first added the result, re-deriving the fact rather than reading a stored value. The justification is a pattern the project's lesson ledger records repeatedly: caveats decay faster than code, so a lesson is not closed until a test enforces it. Section~\ref{sec:transfer} is the rule's sharpest limit --- the tests guard that the test's failure is reported honestly, but nothing had been written down for a test to enforce about the unit of analysis.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Every result comes from simulation, and simulation flatters: noise-free years make thresholds cheap and silence easy. For the null the bias runs the other way --- a clean training year should, if anything, help a discovered net --- but we cannot exclude that real, noisier logs would change the relative standing of conformance against the statistical channels. Three buildings is the harder limit, and it is a limit on the unit of replication rather than on the 73 scenarios or the 4{,}891 evaluable fault days on one system in the deployed scorecard (4{,}540 in the circularity experiment): a building is the unit across which a discovered model must transfer, two of the three are variants of one equipment family, and all share a simulated weather-year family. Any cross-building claim rests on an $n$ so small that the pre-registered test built on it had no attainable significance under any data (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}). The noise-floor gate applies no correction across channels or scenarios (only across devices within the device channel) over roughly 750 binomial tests at $\alpha = 10^{-3}$ (eight channels by 73 scenarios, the device channel run per terminal unit on the fan-powered systems and absent on the single-duct system), for a family-wise error near 0.53 under independence and about 0.75 expected false cells; per-day flags are autocorrelated through weather, so every binomial $p$ is optimistic; and the two conformance channels' false-positive rows are calibration targets rather than out-of-sample measurements. Both caveats cut against the conformance channels looking better than they are, the direction that matters for a null. The evaluation also produced five machine-verified defects in the benchmark datasets, one of which is why Table~\ref{tab:ablation}'s single-duct row carries an adjudication note; they are reported with reproducing evidence in a companion paper~\cite{singh2027erratacompanion}. AI coding agents were used for implementation, analysis scripting and adversarial recomputation; one step depended on them methodologically, the sealed computation of Section~\ref{sec:transfer}, which reports how far that seal actually held. All designs, pre-registrations and claims were authored and approved by the author.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We carried event-log process mining into a domain it had not been applied to, with a pre-registered hypothesis and a falsifier attached, and the falsifier fired. What survives as a detector contains no process mining. Discovery earned its place at design time, by locating an invariant an expert could then write down, and lost it at run time, exactly and measurably, to the rule it had suggested. The three supporting measurements --- a 3.5-point circularity bound, a reversal probe that exposed two nets with fitness above 0.90 as order-blind, and an alignment support that reproduced a raw log count exactly --- are cheap, none is specific to buildings, and each should be measured elsewhere.
