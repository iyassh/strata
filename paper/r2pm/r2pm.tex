\titlerunning{Seven Defects in the LBNL HVAC FDD Benchmarks}
\author{Yassh Singh}

\institute{Thompson Rivers University, Kamloops, Canada}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The LBNL fault-detection datasets are the nearest thing HVAC fault detection has to a common benchmark. We report seven defects, unreported to our knowledge: two families in which one run ships four times under four severity labels, one also mislabelled; one run shipped under two family labels; two columns carrying a per-branch constant that lets a threshold chosen on the fault-free file alone classify every day of every fault file by provenance with zero error; a file whose calendar is rotated by two days; a fault-free file simulated on a different configuration branch than every fault file; and a documentation that places every fan-powered-unit fault in the west zone when only the south zone's columns move. Each ships with machine evidence and a command. Three were found by a hash gate; the battery's four log-generic gates also pass on two public process-mining logs. We place the defects against the event-log imperfection patterns and argue that benchmark-level defects need their own taxonomy.
\keywords{data quality \and event log imperfection \and benchmark leakage \and fault detection}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A benchmark rests on a premise that is rarely checked: that the files are what their names say. In building fault detection and diagnostics (FDD) the evaluation protocols grade an algorithm against seeded faults of known type and severity \cite{frank2018fddevaluation,yuill2013fddprotocol}, so a mislabelled severity or a duplicated run does not add noise; it redefines the quantity scored. And the field rarely re-runs anything: a preprint audit of sixty-five machine-learning HVAC FDD studies found two with a code link, one of them broken \cite{mukhtar2025reproducibility}. A defect in shared data propagates.

Process mining has treated data quality as a stage of its own for a decade: Bose and colleagues on treating log quality before analysis \cite{bose2013dataquality}, Suriadi and colleagues' eleven imperfection patterns \cite{suriadi2017imperfection}, and Weytjens and De~Weerdt on how easily predictive-monitoring benchmarks leak across their splits \cite{weytjens2022dataleakage}. What that literature has had few occasions to address is a leak that is a property of the corpus rather than the split: a class carried by a few files, where any per-file constant becomes a perfect feature. That is the case reported here.

The LBNL FDD datasets \cite{granderson2020lbnlfdddata,granderson2023lbnlfdddata} are simulated annual one-minute data with seeded faults (their creators' lessons-learned account \cite{casillas2024ahufaultmodels} does not report the defects below), released CC BY 4.0 under DOI 10.25984/1881324.\footnote{\url{https://data.openei.org/submissions/5763}, also indexed at \url{https://faultdetection.lbl.gov/data/}.} We use four systems: the single-duct air handler (SDAHU, 21 annual CSVs), the parallel and series fan-powered units (PFPU and SFPU, 31 each, with Brick semantic models) and the fan coil unit (FCU, 49). This paper reports six defects found while preparing a detection study whose process-mining results are in a companion paper \cite{singh2027erpmcompanion}. Three were found by a battery of mechanical gates we now run before a single design decision touches a dataset; one by an early audit; one by auditing our own baseline; one by a hostile audit of the evidence for the first. They remain the largest labelled HVAC fault corpus; that is the argument for repairing them in public.

\section{The Gate Battery}
\label{sec:gates}

Six checks run against the raw files before any science, none requiring insight. \emph{MD5 over raw and converted files}, grouped, so that byte-identical files under different names surface immediately; this produced E1 and E2. \emph{Monotonicity} of timestamps, counting wrap points rather than reporting a boolean. \emph{Calendar identity}: each fault file's timestamp set compared with its fault-free sibling's. \emph{Raw-file rotation}: monotonicity and set identity tested together on the raw CSV, before the conversion step that sorts by timestamp and would mask a rotation; it carries E4's evidence, though an audit found it. \emph{Brick/TTL coverage}: declared points against columns, by name, because a mapping layer that fails open drops points silently. \emph{Log hashing}, applied to the derived event logs downstream of the battery, so that two scenarios reduced to identical logs cannot count as independent evidence.\footnote{Evidence in the scorecards: a \texttt{log\_hash} per scenario and an empty \texttt{event\_identical\_groups} in \texttt{outputs/benchmark\_v6\_*.json}; the audit's zone ground-truth check found no defect and is not counted.} A per-configuration \emph{healthy-silence} gate additionally confirms that a rule set is silent on the fault-free year before it may score anything.


None of the gates was designed in advance; each was added after a defect got past the previous set, and the battery is governed by one rule: a lesson is not closed until a test enforces it. Four of the six gates make no reference to HVAC. To show they are log-generic we ran them unchanged over two public process-mining event logs --- the Sepsis Cases log \cite{mannhardt2016sepsis} and the CoSeLoG receipt-phase log~\cite{buijs2014receipt} --- via pm4py, in under a second each. Both pass; the calendar gate reports Sepsis's ten known midnight-exact stamps.\footnote{\texttt{outputs/gates\_public\_log.json}; \texttt{scripts/gates\_public\_log.py}.} Clean results on curated logs are expected; the point is that the gates cost nothing to run.

Passing on clean logs shows the gates run, not that they catch anything, so we injected each defect class into the Sepsis log and re-ran the four gates:\footnote{\texttt{scripts/gates\_injection.py}, \texttt{outputs/gates\_injection.json}.} a duplicated file fires the hash gate alone, a case copied under a new identifier the trace-hash gate alone, a rotated case monotonicity alone, a case moved forty years the calendar gate alone. A per-case constant encoding the case's outcome --- the log-level analogue of E3 --- fires none of the four: no gate that reads one file at a time can see a relation between an instance and its label (Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy}).

\subsection{E1 and E2 --- two families, one run each}

The four files \texttt{oa\_bias\_-4/-2/2/4\_annual.csv} are byte-identical: one raw MD5 (\texttt{89b13704\ldots}) and one parquet MD5 (\texttt{63b857d7\ldots}) across all four. So are \texttt{coi\_leakage\_010/025/040/050} (\texttt{a9fdfc50\ldots}, \texttt{5133132d\ldots}). The 21-file SDAHU set contains 15 distinct files: the healthy year plus 14 fault scenarios; severity--response analysis for either family is not underpowered but impossible.

\texttt{oa\_bias} is also mislabelled. The recorded \texttt{OA\_TEMP} tracks the healthy file's weather to within $0.33\,^\circ$F (mean $0.02\,^\circ$F, signed mean $\approx 0$, 38\% exact zeros) --- nowhere near the labelled $\pm 2$--$4\,^\circ$F --- and is bit-identical across all fault files, so the labelled sensor-side bias is absent from the data. Yet the run misbehaves relative to the other families in a way consistent with a bias applied to the controller's outdoor-air input rather than to the logged sensor, though observationally indistinguishable from a mislabelled cooling-lockout setpoint. What is proven is narrower and sufficient: the labelled bias is not in the column, the run is faulty, and the four labels are fiction. Published work trained an outdoor-air-bias class on these files \cite{deng2024afgcn}; we assert nothing about that work's results, only that the bias it names is not in the data. Separately, the dataset documentation's file table names the coil-bias family \texttt{sa\_bias\_*\_annual} while the archive ships \texttt{coi\_bias\_*\_annual}: a documentary mismatch in a family we raise no erratum against, recorded in \texttt{ERRATA.md} and not investigated further.

\emph{How they were found.} The hash gate produced both duplicate groups in one pass. The mislabel was overturned twice: the files were first taken for an independent healthy year on the strength of the matching weather column, until a blind residual channel ``false-alarmed'' on them at 48.8\%; a later audit (E5) attributed most of the divergence from the healthy file to the branch offset.

\emph{Consequence and handling.} After the relabel, SDAHU has no independent healthy negative run at all --- a limitation every SDAHU study inherits. Both families are single scenarios in our configuration; \texttt{oa\_bias} is never used as a negative.


\subsection{E3 --- a per-branch constant in two columns}

In the healthy file \texttt{SA\_SPSPT} is constant at 1.60746 and \texttt{SA\_SP} sits near 402; in every one of the twenty fault files \texttt{SA\_SPSPT} is constant at $-400.25253$ and \texttt{SA\_SP} lies between 1.10 and 1.15 in per-file mean of day-medians. An earlier version of this erratum read that as units and roles swapped. It is not: the two branches differ by a single additive constant. $1.60746 - (-400.25253) = 401.85999$, and the healthy file's \texttt{SA\_SP} with the fan off minus the fault files' with the fan off is again $401.86$. Subtracting that constant from the healthy \texttt{SA\_SP} reproduces the fault files' trace to three decimals; the old unit reading implied a duct at $1.6$ inH$_2$O with the fan off, which is impossible. The healthy branch has the constant added to \texttt{SA\_SP}; the fault branch has it subtracted from \texttt{SA\_SPSPT}. Because the offset is branch-specific and E5 establishes that the branches differ, E3 is most likely a symptom of the same divergence, listed separately for its distinct consequence and not counted as independent evidence.

\emph{How it was found.} By auditing our own PCA baseline, whose error statistic had reached $10^{37}$ on exactly these columns; the audit gate then checks the healthy file against one fault file, and the extension to all twenty is in the leakage artefact of Section~\ref{sec:leak}, which carries every file's range.

\emph{Handling.} Both columns are in an explicit erratum list that every baseline drops; our detector configurations never mapped them.


\subsection{E4 --- a date-rotated calendar}

The raw file \texttt{SFPU\_SensorBias\_RMTEMP\_-2C.csv} is a pure rotation by exactly 2{,}880 rows, two days at one-minute sampling: its timestamp set is identical to the fault-free file's, the series is non-monotonic with exactly one wrap point, and its first row is 2018-01-03 00:00 where the fault-free file's is 2018-01-01 00:00. It is the file-granularity analogue of Suriadi and colleagues' \emph{inadvertent time travel} \cite{suriadi2017imperfection}, except that the timestamps are correct and the order is not, so a reader that sorts by timestamp never sees it and positional comparison does.

\emph{How it was found.} By an early audit, not a gate: the monotonicity gate runs on converted files that conversion has already sorted. The raw-file rotation gate was built four phases later so the defect has machine evidence and cannot recur --- a gate must sit upstream of the preprocessing that masks it. \emph{Consequence and handling.} Positional comparison against the healthy file misaligns every timestamp for a year; the scenario is excluded, leaving SFPU with 29 scored scenarios, and sort-on-convert plus the calendar-identity gate are permanent.


\subsection{E5 --- the healthy file is on a different configuration branch}

Three independent measurements agree that the SDAHU fault-free file was simulated on a different configuration branch than every fault file. \emph{Damper floor:} during fan-on hours the healthy file's outdoor-air damper minimum is 0.000; every fault file floors at exactly 0.100. \emph{Schedule:} on the pipeline's own occupancy signal the day universe is identical between branches but the healthy file's first occupied minute is 05:01--05:02 on 200 of those days (06:01 on 33 and 06:02 on 70 of the rest) where every fault file's is 06:01 on every day.\footnote{\texttt{outputs/e5\_schedule.json}; this leg was prose only until that artefact was added, which is itself an instance of the rule below.} An earlier version's occupied-row and runtime figures are retracted. \emph{Simulation-physics offset:} the residual channel's no-fault baseline differs by $-1.06\,^\circ$F between the branches ($+0.008\,^\circ$F on the fault branch, from five concordant measurements, against $+1.071\,^\circ$F on the healthy), uniform across occupied hours and surviving restriction of the healthy file to the fault branch's schedule.\footnote{\texttt{outputs/x11\_branch.json}.}

\emph{How it was found.} By a hostile audit of E1's evidence. It had hidden for six phases because every comparison ran against the healthy file and no fault-versus-fault control existed; the standing rule now is that healthy-versus-fault divergence is fault evidence only after such a control.

\emph{Consequence and handling.} Every healthy-versus-fault comparison on SDAHU carries the branch offset on top of the fault. Fault-versus-fault divergence is $0.05$--$0.14\,^\circ$F, an order of magnitude below the offset, so most of E1's degree-scale divergence belongs to the branch. In our own benchmark the \texttt{oa\_bias} residual detection sits on the fault-branch baseline ($+0.007$ against $+0.008$): it is branch provenance, and under a pre-registered branch-sensitivity adjudication the SDAHU scorecard is 13 of 14 rather than 14 of 14. The \texttt{coi\_bias} detections survive by an order of magnitude; the nine rules-carried scenarios are branch-immune, a signature rule comparing position to command within one file.

\section{E3 as a Measured Leak}
\label{sec:leak}

E1, E2, E4 and E6 corrupt an accounting; E3 hands every data-driven model a free discriminator, and we measured how free: one class, healthy, is one file, the other twenty. A threshold chosen from the healthy file alone (the midpoint between its minimum day-median and zero) classifies 365 of 365 healthy days and 7{,}150 of 7{,}150 fault-file days correctly on each column: accuracy 1.0, every fault file's range on the far side.\footnote{\texttt{outputs/e3\_leakage.json}; \texttt{scripts/e3\_leakage.py}. Healthy \texttt{SA\_SP} day-medians span $[401.85, 403.48]$; fault-file day-medians span $[-0.60, 5.97]$.} A decision stump exploits this as completely as a deep network.

We know it is exploited in practice because we caught ourselves. Our principal-component baseline, fitted on the healthy file and scored on the fault files, reached a squared prediction error of order $10^{37}$, a number with no physical reading and the reason the baseline was audited rather than celebrated; the two columns were the entire explanation. This is leakage in Kapoor and Narayanan's sense \cite{kapoor2023leakage} --- a feature that proxies the label through the data's construction --- but not the split leakage of Weytjens and De~Weerdt \cite{weytjens2022dataleakage}: here the class is confounded with the file, so any column constant within a file is a label. Wherever a class is carried by few files, every within-file constant deserves this check.

We assert a property of the data, not of any published result: any model trained on healthy and scored on fault files with these columns in its feature set has a perfect provenance discriminator on this system. Published SDAHU results that used the full column set should be read with this in mind; the remedy is to re-run without the two columns and report both numbers.

\section{Where the Event-Log Taxonomy Ends}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

Four of the six have loose homes in the existing taxonomies. In Bose and colleagues' grid of attribute against problem type \cite{bose2013dataquality}, E4 sits in the incorrect-position cell and E1's mislabel in the incorrect-activity-name cell; E1, E2 and E6 are corpus-level analogues of Suriadi and colleagues' synonymous labels \cite{suriadi2017imperfection} --- different names for one content --- at the granularity of files rather than events. E3 and E5 are outside both taxonomies: one is a leaked feature, the other a confounded provenance, and both are properties of how a corpus was assembled rather than of any log within it. The patterns were built for a single log extracted from one system; a benchmark is a set of files whose \emph{relationships} carry the labels, and that is where these defects live. We propose a first cut, four classes named by the relation each corrupts: \emph{duplicated instances} (E1, E2, E6; the copied case of Section~\ref{sec:gates}) --- two labels, one content; \emph{per-instance constants} (E3; the leaked outcome code) --- a feature constant within an instance that varies with its label; \emph{confounded provenance} (E5; cf.\ split leakage across train and test~\cite{weytjens2022dataleakage}) --- the partition carries a difference that is not the one under study; \emph{misaligned instances} (E4) --- two instances compared position by position are not aligned. The first and fourth are caught by log-generic gates; the second and third cannot be by any gate that reads one file at a time --- which is what ``the taxonomies end here'' means.

\section{Recommendations}
\label{sec:recs}

For maintainers: an errata notice at the distribution points; drop or flag \texttt{SA\_SP} and \texttt{SA\_SPSPT}; republish or relabel the four \texttt{oa\_bias} and \texttt{coi\_leakage} runs; republish the rotated file in order; record the configuration branch; ship checksums. For users (the log-generic gates ship as \texttt{strata gates LOG.xes}): hash and group files first; test rotation on the raw files; build a fault-versus-fault control into every design; ask of every feature whether it is constant within a file; report scenario counts as distinct runs (14 on SDAHU, not 20; 29 on SFPU, not 30; 47 on FCU, not 48). For reviewers: two questions --- which columns, and how many distinct files per class --- would have caught E3.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Seven defects in the most widely used public HVAC FDD benchmarks were found not by insight but by mechanical gates and audits run before and during a detection study: three by the hash gate, one by an early audit, one by auditing our own baseline, one by a hostile audit of the evidence for another. Each would have inflated a published result; one, a per-branch constant that makes two columns a perfect provenance discriminator, is now measured rather than asserted. Of five LBNL archives three carry a defect; the dual-duct AHU passed every gate and the fan coil unit failed the hash gate at once (E6). The datasets remain the best the field has, which is why a defect in them is a multiplier that no downstream replication will catch.
