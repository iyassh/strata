\institute{Thompson Rivers University, Kamloops, Canada}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The LBNL fault-detection datasets are the nearest thing HVAC fault detection has to a common benchmark. We report five defects, unreported to our knowledge: two families in which one simulation run ships four times under four severity labels, one also mislabelled; two columns carrying a per-branch constant that lets a threshold chosen on the fault-free file alone classify every day of every fault file by provenance with zero error; a file whose calendar is rotated by two days; and a fault-free file simulated on a different configuration branch than every fault file. Each ships with machine evidence and a command. Two were found by the hash gate of a battery assembled defect by defect that now runs before any analysis; its four log-generic gates also pass on two public process-mining logs. We place the defects against the event-log imperfection patterns and argue that benchmark-level defects need their own taxonomy.
\keywords{data quality \and event log imperfection \and benchmark leakage \and fault detection}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A benchmark is an argument about what a method can do, and it rests on a premise that is almost never checked: that the files are what their names say they are. In building fault detection and diagnostics (FDD) that premise carries unusual weight, because the evaluation protocols grade an algorithm against seeded faults of known type and severity \cite{frank2018fddevaluation,yuill2013fddprotocol}, so a mislabelled severity, a duplicated run or a corrupted column does not add noise --- it redefines the quantity being scored. The field is also poorly placed to catch such problems downstream: a preprint audit of sixty-five machine-learning HVAC FDD studies found two that shared a code link, one of them broken, and 72\% that did not state whether their data was public \cite{mukhtar2025reproducibility}. A defect in shared data is not corrected by replication where there is none. It propagates.

Process mining has taken data quality seriously for a decade. Bose and colleagues argued that event-log quality issues must be treated before any analysis \cite{bose2013dataquality}; Suriadi and colleagues catalogued eleven event-log imperfection patterns and a systematic approach to cleaning them \cite{suriadi2017imperfection}; the PM$^2$ methodology places data extraction and preparation as a stage with its own checks \cite{vaneck2015pm2}; and Weytjens and De~Weerdt showed how easily public benchmarks for predictive process monitoring leak across their splits \cite{weytjens2022dataleakage}. What that literature has not yet had many occasions to address is the case where the leak is a property of the corpus rather than the split --- where a class is carried by a small number of files and any per-file constant becomes a perfect feature. That is the case reported here.

The LBNL FDD datasets \cite{granderson2020lbnlfdddata} are simulated annual one-minute data with seeded faults, released CC BY 4.0 under DOI 10.25984/1881324.\footnote{\url{https://data.openei.org/submissions/5763}, also indexed at \url{https://faultdetection.lbl.gov/data/}.} We use three systems: the single-duct air handler (SDAHU, 21 annual CSVs) and the parallel and series fan-powered units (PFPU and SFPU, 31 each, with Brick semantic models). Published work trains on these files; an adaptive-fusion graph convolutional network, for one, took its outdoor-air sensor-bias class from the SDAHU \texttt{oa\_bias} files \cite{deng2024afgcn}. This paper reports five defects found while preparing a detection study whose process-mining results are in a companion paper \cite{singh2027erpmcompanion}. Three were found by a battery of mechanical gates we now run before a single design decision touches a dataset; one by auditing our own baseline; one by a hostile audit of the evidence for the first. The datasets remain the largest labelled HVAC fault corpus available. That is the argument for repairing them in public.

\section{The Gate Battery}
\label{sec:gates}

Six checks run against the raw files before any science, none requiring insight. \emph{MD5 over raw and converted files}, grouped, so that byte-identical files under different names surface immediately; this produced E1 and E2. \emph{Monotonicity} of timestamps, counting wrap points rather than reporting a boolean. \emph{Calendar identity}: each fault file's timestamp set compared with its fault-free sibling's. \emph{Raw-file rotation}: monotonicity and set identity tested together on the raw CSV, before the conversion step that sorts by timestamp and would mask a rotation; this, not the monotonicity gate, produced E4. \emph{Brick/TTL coverage}: on both fan-powered systems, 109 declared points against 109 columns, with one name that does not cross over (\texttt{RFSFSPD} against \texttt{RF\%SFSPD}), reported by name because a mapping layer that fails open would silently drop a point. \emph{Log hashing}, applied to the derived event logs downstream of the battery, so that two scenarios reduced to identical logs cannot count as independent evidence.\footnote{Its evidence lives in the scorecards, not in \texttt{week0\_audit.json}: \texttt{outputs/benchmark\_v6\_*.json} carries a \texttt{log\_hash} per scenario and an empty \texttt{event\_identical\_groups}. The audit artefact also carries a zone ground-truth check, which found no defect and is not counted here.} A per-configuration \emph{healthy-silence} gate additionally confirms that a rule set is silent on the fault-free year before it may score anything.

\begin{verbatim}
uv run python scripts/02_week0_audit.py all
uv run python scripts/03_healthy_silence.py configs/lbnl_pfpu \
    data/processed/pfpu/PFPU_FaultFree.parquet
\end{verbatim}

None of the gates was designed in advance; each was added after a defect got past the previous set, and the battery is governed by a rule learned when a documented lesson was re-learned two phases later: a lesson is not closed until a test enforces it. Four of the six gates make no reference to HVAC. To show they are log-generic rather than corpus-specific we ran them, unchanged in logic, over two public process-mining event logs --- the Sepsis Cases log \cite{mannhardt2016sepsis}, 1{,}050 cases and 15{,}214 events, and the public receipt-phase log of the CoSeLoG project~\cite{buijs2014receipt}, 1{,}434 cases and 8{,}577 events --- via pm4py in under a second each on a laptop; the timing is printed by the script and not committed, being machine-dependent. Both pass: no case with non-monotonic timestamps, no pair of cases with identical activity-and-timestamp sequences, no duplicate files; the trace-hash gate separates ordinary activity variants (846 and 116) from true duplicates (none), and the calendar gate reports ten midnight-exact timestamps in Sepsis, a known property of that log.\footnote{\texttt{outputs/gates\_public\_log.json}; \texttt{scripts/gates\_public\_log.py}.} Clean results on well-curated logs are the expected outcome; the point is that the gates that found three defects in an HVAC corpus cost nothing to run on an event log.

Passing on clean logs shows the gates run, not that they catch anything, so we injected each defect class into the Sepsis log, one at a time, and re-ran the four gates.\footnote{\texttt{scripts/gates\_injection.py}, \texttt{outputs/gates\_injection.json}.} The file shipped again under another name fires the hash gate and nothing else; one case copied under a new identifier fires the trace-hash gate alone; one case's timestamps rotated by one position fires monotonicity alone; one case moved forty years out of the log's era fires the calendar gate alone. A per-case constant that encodes the case's outcome --- the log-level analogue of E3 --- fires none of the four, and no gate that reads one file at a time can see it, because the defect is a relation between an instance and its label (Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy}). Each run takes a few seconds on the 15{,}214-event log.

\subsection{E1 and E2 --- two families, one run each}

The four files \texttt{oa\_bias\_-4/-2/2/4\_annual.csv} are byte-identical: one raw MD5 (\texttt{89b13704\ldots}) and one parquet MD5 (\texttt{63b857d7\ldots}) across all four. So are \texttt{coi\_leakage\_010/025/040/050} (\texttt{a9fdfc50\ldots}, \texttt{5133132d\ldots}). The severity labels carry no information, and the 21-file SDAHU set contains 15 distinct files: the healthy year plus 14 fault scenarios. Severity--response analysis for either family is not underpowered but impossible.

\texttt{oa\_bias} is also mislabelled. The recorded \texttt{OA\_TEMP} tracks the healthy file's weather to within $0.33\,^\circ$F (mean $0.02\,^\circ$F, signed mean $\approx 0$, 38\% exact zeros) --- nowhere near the labelled $\pm 2$--$4\,^\circ$F --- and is bit-identical across all fault files, so the labelled sensor-side bias is absent from the data. Yet the run misbehaves relative to the other families in a way consistent with a bias applied to the controller's outdoor-air input rather than to the logged sensor, though observationally indistinguishable from a mislabelled cooling-lockout setpoint. What is proven is narrower and sufficient: the labelled bias is not in the column, the run is faulty, and the four labels are fiction. Published work trained an outdoor-air-bias class on these files \cite{deng2024afgcn}; we assert nothing about that work's results, only that the bias it names is not in the data. Separately, the dataset documentation's file table names the coil-bias family \texttt{sa\_bias\_*\_annual} while the archive ships \texttt{coi\_bias\_*\_annual}: a documentary mismatch in a family we raise no erratum against, recorded in \texttt{ERRATA.md} and not investigated further.

\emph{How they were found.} The hash gate produced both duplicate groups in one pass. The mislabel was overturned twice: the files were first treated as an independent healthy year on the strength of the matching weather column, until a blind residual channel ``false-alarmed'' on them at 48.8\% and the re-examination found a genuine fault; a later audit (E5) attributed most of the divergence against the healthy file to the branch offset.

\emph{Consequence and handling.} After the relabel, SDAHU has no independent healthy negative run at all --- a limitation every SDAHU study inherits. Both families are single scenarios in our configuration; \texttt{oa\_bias} is never used as a negative.


\subsection{E3 --- a per-branch constant in two columns}

In the healthy file \texttt{SA\_SPSPT} is constant at 1.60746 and \texttt{SA\_SP} sits near 402; in every one of the twenty fault files \texttt{SA\_SPSPT} is constant at $-400.25253$ and \texttt{SA\_SP} lies between 1.10 and 1.15 in per-file mean of day-medians. An earlier version of this erratum read that as units and roles swapped. It is not: the two branches differ by a single additive constant. $1.60746 - (-400.25253) = 401.85999$, and the healthy file's \texttt{SA\_SP} with the fan off minus the fault files' with the fan off is again $401.86$. Subtracting that constant from the healthy \texttt{SA\_SP} reproduces the fault files' trace to three decimals; the old unit reading implied a duct at $1.6$ inH$_2$O with the fan off, which is impossible. The healthy branch has the constant added to \texttt{SA\_SP}; the fault branch has it subtracted from \texttt{SA\_SPSPT}. One column is corrupted per branch, a different column each. Because the offset is branch-specific and E5 establishes that the branches differ, E3 is most likely a symptom of the same divergence; it is listed separately because its consequence is distinct, and the two should not be counted as independent evidence.

\emph{How it was found.} By auditing our own PCA baseline, whose error statistic had reached $10^{37}$ on exactly these columns; the audit gate then checks the healthy file against one fault file, and the extension to all twenty is in the leakage artefact of Section~\ref{sec:leak}, which carries every file's range.

\emph{Handling.} Both columns are in an explicit erratum list that every baseline drops; our detector configurations never mapped them.


\subsection{E4 --- a date-rotated calendar}

The raw file \texttt{SFPU\_SensorBias\_RMTEMP\_-2C.csv} is a pure rotation by exactly 2{,}880 rows, two days at one-minute sampling: its timestamp set is identical to the fault-free file's, the series is non-monotonic with exactly one wrap point, and its first row is 2018-01-03 00:00 where the fault-free file's is 2018-01-01 00:00. The rotation is invisible to every summary statistic and to any check of the calendar's contents; it lives in row order alone. It is the file-granularity analogue of Suriadi and colleagues' \emph{inadvertent time travel} \cite{suriadi2017imperfection}, though the corruption differs: there the timestamp values are wrong, here they are correct and the row order is not, so a log reader that sorts by timestamp never sees it and only positional comparison does.

\emph{How it was found.} By an audit in an early phase, not by a gate: the monotonicity gate then in place runs on converted files, and our conversion step sorts by timestamp, so it could never have exhibited the defect. The raw-file rotation gate was built four phases later so that the defect has machine evidence and cannot recur --- the reason a gate must sit upstream of the preprocessing that would mask it. \emph{Consequence and handling.} Positional comparison against the healthy file misaligns every timestamp for a year; the scenario is excluded, leaving SFPU with 29 scored scenarios, and sort-on-convert plus the calendar-identity gate are permanent.


\subsection{E5 --- the healthy file is on a different configuration branch}

Three independent measurements agree that the SDAHU fault-free file was simulated on a different configuration branch than every fault file. \emph{Damper floor:} during fan-on hours the healthy file's outdoor-air damper minimum is 0.000; every fault file floors at exactly 0.100 (the three \texttt{damper\_stuck} files at 0.25, 0.75 and 1.0 sit at their stuck value). \emph{Schedule:} on the pipeline's own occupancy signal the day universe is identical between branches --- 303 occupied days on every full-year file, within two minutes per day --- but the healthy file's first occupied minute is 05:01--05:02 on 200 of those days where every fault file's is 06:01 on every day.\footnote{\texttt{outputs/e5\_schedule.json}; this leg was prose only until that artefact was added, which is itself an instance of the rule below.} An earlier version of this erratum quoted a range of occupied-row counts and a daily runtime difference instead; both were measured on a sensor the pipeline does not read and are retracted. \emph{Simulation-physics offset:} the residual channel's no-fault baseline differs by $-1.06\,^\circ$F between the branches --- $+0.008\,^\circ$F on the fault branch, from five concordant measurements, against $+1.071\,^\circ$F on the healthy branch with a band of $[0.526, 1.372]$ --- uniform across occupied hours and surviving restriction of the healthy file to the fault branch's schedule.\footnote{\texttt{outputs/x11\_branch.json}.}

\emph{How it was found.} By a hostile audit assigned to attack E1's evidence. It had hidden for six phases for a structural reason: every comparison ran against the healthy file, and no fault-versus-fault control existed. The standing rule it produced is that healthy-versus-fault divergence is fault evidence only after a fault-versus-fault control.

\emph{Consequence and handling.} Every healthy-versus-fault comparison on SDAHU carries the branch offset on top of the fault. Fault-versus-fault cross-family divergence is $0.05$--$0.14\,^\circ$F, an order of magnitude below the offset, so most of the degree-scale divergence quoted against the healthy file in E1 belongs to the branch. In our own benchmark the \texttt{oa\_bias} residual detection sits on the fault-branch baseline ($+0.007$ against $+0.008$): it is branch provenance, and under a pre-registered branch-sensitivity adjudication the SDAHU scorecard is 13 of 14 rather than 14 of 14. The \texttt{coi\_bias} detections survive by an order of magnitude, and the nine rules-carried scenarios are branch-immune because a signature rule compares a device's position to its own command within one file. This erratum bites our own benchmark, and we say so.

\section{E3 as a Measured Leak}
\label{sec:leak}

E1, E2 and E4 corrupt an accounting. E3 hands every data-driven model a free discriminator, and we measured how free. One class, healthy, is one file; the other is twenty. For each column we chose a threshold from the healthy file alone --- the midpoint between its minimum day-median and zero, consulting no fault file --- and classified every day of every file. On \texttt{SA\_SP} the rule classifies 365 of 365 healthy days as healthy and misclassifies 0 of 7{,}150 fault-file days; on \texttt{SA\_SPSPT}, the same. Accuracy is 1.0 on both columns, and every fault file's range lies entirely on the far side of the threshold.\footnote{\texttt{outputs/e3\_leakage.json}; \texttt{scripts/e3\_leakage.py}. Healthy \texttt{SA\_SP} day-medians span $[401.85, 403.48]$; fault-file day-medians span $[-0.60, 5.97]$.} A decision stump exploits this as completely as a deep network.

We know it is exploited in practice because we caught ourselves. Our principal-component baseline, fitted on the healthy file and scored on the fault files, reached a squared prediction error of order $10^{37}$, a number with no physical reading and the reason the baseline was audited rather than celebrated; the two columns were the entire explanation. This is leakage in the sense Kapoor and Narayanan catalogue for machine-learning science \cite{kapoor2023leakage} --- a feature that proxies the label through the data's construction --- and its structure differs from the split leakage Weytjens and De~Weerdt address in process-mining benchmarks \cite{weytjens2022dataleakage}: here the class is confounded with the file, so any column constant within a file is a label. Wherever a class is carried by few files, every within-file constant deserves this check.

We assert a property of the data, not of any published result: any model trained on healthy and scored on fault files with these columns in its feature set has a perfect provenance discriminator available to it on this system. Published SDAHU machine-learning results that used the full column set should be read with this in mind; the remedy for an author is to re-run without the two columns and report both numbers.

\section{Where the Event-Log Taxonomy Ends}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

Three of the five have homes, if loose ones, in the existing taxonomies. In Bose and colleagues' grid of attribute against problem type \cite{bose2013dataquality}, E4 sits in the incorrect-position cell and E1's mislabel in the incorrect-activity-name cell; E1 and E2 are corpus-level analogues of Suriadi and colleagues' synonymous labels \cite{suriadi2017imperfection} --- different names for one content --- at the granularity of files rather than events, and our own trace-hash gate, which finds cases with identical activity and timestamp sequences, is their exact event-log counterpart. E3 and E5 are outside both taxonomies: one is a leaked feature, the other a confounded provenance, and both are properties of how a corpus was assembled rather than of any log within it. The patterns were built for a single log extracted from one system; a benchmark is a set of files whose \emph{relationships} carry the labels, and that is where these defects live. We propose the first cut of one, with four classes named by the relation each corrupts. \emph{Duplicated instances} (E1, E2; the copied case of Section~\ref{sec:gates}): two labels, one content --- label-to-instance. \emph{Per-instance constants} (E3; the leaked outcome code of Section~\ref{sec:gates}): a feature constant within an instance that varies with its label --- feature-to-label. \emph{Confounded provenance} (E5; and the split-level leakage that Weytjens and De Weerdt document when cases straddle train and test~\cite{weytjens2022dataleakage}): the partition that defines a comparison also carries a difference that is not the one under study --- partition-to-condition. \emph{Misaligned instances} (E4): two instances meant to be compared position by position are not --- instance-to-instance. The first and fourth are caught by the log-generic gates; the second and third are not, and cannot be by any gate that reads one file at a time, which is the operational content of saying that the event-log taxonomies end here. It would give the gate battery a principled basis instead of the accidental one it has.

\section{Recommendations}
\label{sec:recs}

For maintainers: publish an errata notice at the distribution points, not only in a paper; drop or flag \texttt{SA\_SP} and \texttt{SA\_SPSPT} on SDAHU; republish the four \texttt{oa\_bias} and \texttt{coi\_leakage} runs or withdraw their severity labels; republish the rotated file in calendar order; record the configuration branch in metadata; and ship per-file checksums. For users (the four log-generic gates ship as \texttt{strata gates LOG.xes}, the injection experiment as a script): hash and group the files first; test rotation on the raw files with monotonicity and set identity together; build a fault-versus-fault control into every design; ask of every feature whether it is constant within a file; and report scenario counts as distinct runs --- 14 on SDAHU, not 20; 29 on SFPU, not 30. For reviewers: which columns were used, and how many distinct files backed each class, are two questions that cost nothing and would have caught E3 in any paper that used it.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Five defects in the most widely used public HVAC FDD benchmarks were found not by insight but by mechanical gates and audits run before and during a detection study: two by the hash gate of a six-gate battery whose log-generic half runs in under a second on public event logs, one by an early audit, one by auditing our own baseline, one by a hostile audit of the evidence for another. Every one of them would have inflated a published result, and one of them --- a per-branch constant that turns two columns into a perfect provenance discriminator --- is now measured rather than asserted. The datasets remain the best the field has, which is precisely why a defect in them is a multiplier, and in a subfield where almost nothing is re-run, a multiplier that no downstream replication will catch.
