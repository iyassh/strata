\begin{abstract}
Faults and poor control in heating, ventilation and air-conditioning (HVAC) equipment waste 15 to 30 percent of a commercial building's energy, and many go unnoticed. Building automation systems log the operating data and display it as trend graphs, so a fault is found only when a technician reads the graphs. Automated tools either apply rules written in advance, which miss any fault that was not anticipated, or report anomaly scores that say a day is unusual without saying where or what is wrong. This paper presents STRATA, a framework that works from the trend data a building already records and learns, from one period of normal, fault-free operation, what each day should look like. It then examines every new day with eight checks, each asking whether a physical relationship or an operating sequence has moved outside its normal range. Each check is first run on fault-free days it has never seen. The number of those days it wrongly flags is its false-alarm rate. A check may then raise an alarm only when it flags far more days than that false-alarm rate could explain. Every alarm states what broke, where, the evidence and the check's measured false-alarm rate. Process mining supplies the structure. The sensor data becomes a log of events along the control sequence, and models of normal operation are discovered from that log for each zone's equipment and for the whole unit, so every alarm knows which piece of equipment it belongs to. On five public benchmark systems from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, each simulated with faults whose cause and location are known, STRATA detected 158 of 175 faults, most on the first day, and raised false alarms on 26 of 480 fault-free days it had never seen. In the 50 cases where the fault lay inside one zone's equipment, the alarm pointed to the correct zone in 37, to no zone in 13, and to a wrong zone in none. Moving the framework to a new building means writing one configuration file, not new code. The planned next step is a live trial on a real building. It is also the first step toward ProcessHeal, a self-healing building that finds its own faults, locates them, works out their cause and corrects them. STRATA does the finding and the locating; working out the cause and correcting it are the later stages.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Buildings account for about a third of energy-related carbon emissions \cite{unep2025gsr}, and HVAC accounts for up to half of a building's energy use \cite{bi2024aihvacreview}. Faults and poor control that go uncorrected waste 15 to 30 percent of a commercial building's energy \cite{katipamula2005fddreview1}. A study of more than 60,000 pieces of HVAC equipment found 40 percent of air handlers in a faulted state on any given day, in buildings that already had automation systems with alarms \cite{crowe2023faultprevalence}. Seven of the ten most common air-handler faults in that study are faults of behaviour, such as a wrong schedule or an unreleased manual override, rather than a broken part.

A building operator today usually learns of such a fault in one of three ways. An occupant complains that a room is too hot or too cold. The operator notices that a line on a trend graph has drifted from where it usually sits. Or an automated detector raises an alarm. The first two find the fault late and depend on someone happening to look. The third works only as well as the method behind it.

Those automated methods fall into two groups. Rule libraries, which come built into commercial building automation systems, are transparent and need no training data, but they catch only the faults for which someone wrote a rule. Machine-learning detectors learn from the building's own data and so can find faults that no rule anticipated, but most report an anomaly score: a number that says today is unusual without saying where or what is wrong. A score is not enough to write a work order.

This paper presents STRATA. It is configured with rules, written once per building in a single file, but those rules do not raise alarms. They turn the sensor data into a log of events: cooling started, damper open while commanded closed, a supply-air reading that physics says cannot be right. What a normal day of those events looks like is then learned from a period of the building's own fault-free operation, and every new day is checked against it, so a fault can show up as a departure from normal even when no rule names it. Each alarm states the fault, the piece of equipment, the evidence, and how often that same check has been wrong on fault-free days.

The method behind it is process mining, which discovers a model of a process from a log of events and checks later behaviour against the model \cite{vanderaalst2016processmining}. STRATA builds such a log from the sensor data, for each zone's equipment and for the whole unit. That is how an alarm knows where it belongs, and why it can check the order of control actions as well as physical relationships. To our knowledge this is the first use of process mining for HVAC fault detection: the surveys of both fields contain no such entry, and our own search found none (Section~\ref{sec:related}). On five public benchmark systems with 175 seeded faults, STRATA detected 158, most on the first day, and placed no alarm in a wrong zone (Section~\ref{sec:results}). The code and the complete evaluation are public.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/iyassh/strata}}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Rule-based detection, the method in use today}

Fault detection in real buildings is almost entirely rule-based. The air-handling-unit performance assessment rules of Schein and colleagues set the pattern \cite{schein2006apar}. A rule states a relationship that must hold in a given operating mode, such as no water flow through a closed valve, and fires when it does not. ASHRAE Guideline 36 publishes such fault conditions alongside its control sequences \cite{ashrae2021guideline36,ashrae2024guideline36}, and an open-source implementation of them exists \cite{openfdd}. A survey of fourteen commercial fault-detection tools found this rule-and-threshold style to be the norm \cite{granderson2017fddtoolsurvey}, and interviews with 29 industry experts in 2024 found it still so \cite{heimarandersen2024barriers}.

Detection pays when it is used. Among 26 organisations running fault-detection software on 550 buildings, the median energy saving was 8 percent \cite{lin2020achievedsavings}. Yet having the rules installed is not the same as using them. In a two-site field trial of Guideline 36, the facility team paid no attention to the built-in fault detection \cite{zhou2025g36field}. A 2026 California code study names alarm overload and a shortage of trained staff as the main barriers \cite{case2026fdd}, and when alarms outnumber the people who can act on them, false alarms erode trust in the true ones \cite{diallo2026alarmfatigue}.

Rules work from the first day and need no baseline data. Their limit is structural: they catch only the faults someone wrote a rule for, and miss any fault that no rule author anticipated. The Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) group itself notes that commercial diagnostics often list several candidate causes or none \cite{lin2025activemultimode}, and recent work answers that with active tests that command the equipment and watch the response \cite{parsaei2025proactive}. The same group has carried the rule approach through to correction, field-testing seven hand-coded correction algorithms on 225 pieces of equipment in four buildings \cite{lin2020faultcorrection,pritoni2022faultcorrection}. Section~\ref{sec:results} measures both the strength and the limit of rules against Guideline 36 on the same benchmarks.

\subsection{Machine learning, and what its alarms leave out}

Hundreds of studies have applied machine learning to HVAC fault detection. A 2024 systematic review covers 211 of them \cite{bi2024aihvacreview}, and a 2025 review follows the field through the equipment lifecycle \cite{wang2025lifecyclereview}. On the standard benchmarks, accuracy has saturated at 97 to 98 percent \cite{tun2021hybridrfsvm,prince2025improved1dcnnlstm}. What has not been solved is the output. A classifier or an anomaly score says that something is wrong. Where an explanation is offered, it comes from a separate layer that ranks the sensors by how much each one swayed the verdict \cite{deng2024afgcn,li2024interpretablegnn,wang2025xmhac}. That answers which signal mattered, not what the equipment should have been doing. Two 2026 studies come closest to a located alarm. One estimates the position of a stuck damper or valve after training on fault-free data alone \cite{park2026koopman}. The other weighs the evidence for each component that could be at fault and names the most likely one, without labels \cite{paolini2026bayesian}.

The second problem is labels. Real buildings have none, so the strongest recent work trains only on normal operation and flags whatever departs from it \cite{abdollah2024transformerssl,elmokhtari2024aebas}, and what it reports is a single deviation score. A model learned on one building also travels badly to another \cite{zhu2021chillertransfer,ghalamsiah2025canhvac}: in a 2026 study, detectors that performed well inside their own buildings lost almost half of that performance on buildings they had not seen \cite{jiang2026crossdomain}. A live deployment on a Danish school building chose its detector for the fewest false alarms \cite{melgaard2025walkthrough}, and an audit of 65 such studies found two that shared their code \cite{mukhtar2025reproducibility}.

Combining the two groups is not new. Two recent hybrids use hand-written rules to label historical data and then train a classifier on those labels: one on ten months of data from six offices at a Danish university \cite{mazzetto2025danishbas}, the other on six months from a real air handler in Norway, with the Schein rules as the rule layer \cite{zabadi2026digitaltwinapar}. The rules are still written in advance, so the classifier can learn only the faults the rules already name, and the output is still a class label without a location. A newer direction has a large language model write and refine the rules under physical constraints and report its evidence; it beats the neural detectors on the LBNL air-handler data \cite{linhua2025pillm}, although a 2026 review of 66 such studies found none in sustained deployment \cite{neubauer2026llmreview}.

\subsection{Process mining, and how close it has come to buildings}

Process mining discovers a model of a process from a log of events and checks later behaviour against that model \cite{vanderaalst2016processmining}. The discovery algorithm used here, the inductive miner, is the standard one \cite{leemans2013inductiveminer}. The check lines a day's events up against the model and shows where they depart from it \cite{carmona2018conformancechecking}, so the explanation comes with the method rather than from a layer added afterwards. Checking conformance has served as an anomaly detector on event logs for a decade \cite{bezerra2013anomalytraces}. Applying it to sensor data needs one extra step, event abstraction, which turns continuous signals into discrete events \cite{janssen2021sensoreventlog,brzychczy2025pmsensorreview}. How to do that well, and how to keep the resulting events checkable by a person, is an open question in the process-mining community as of 2026 \cite{tratnjek2026signalsevents,imenkamp2026glassbox}.

With that step the method has reached several physical systems. It detects anomalies in chemical plants \cite{shi2024processanomaly} and in industrial alarm sequences \cite{norouzifar2026alarmsequences}. One group builds digital twins that learn normal behaviour offline and check it online \cite{vitale2025digitaltwin}, diagnoses faults on a robotic arm with few misses and few false alarms \cite{vitale2025roadcps}, and shows that features drawn from the process model make a detector far better than the plain check alone \cite{vitale2025kbsfeatures}. The method also validates sensor data in industrial Internet-of-Things systems \cite{husom2026udava}. The closest analogue to a building is pasteurisation, where sensor signals are abstracted into activities and checked against the critical control points of the HACCP food-safety standard \cite{moradbeikie2025pasteurization}; the parallel between those control points and Guideline 36 control sequences is the one STRATA builds on. Two kinds of work sound similar but are not this. Petri nets of HVAC equipment drawn by hand exist \cite{deabes2023fuzzyptn}, but a net an engineer draws is not a model discovered from a log. And LBNL's OpenBuildingControl verifies that a control implementation conforms to its Guideline 36 specification before the building runs \cite{wetter2022obc}; STRATA checks what the building does afterwards, from its operating data.

\subsection{The gap, and where STRATA sits}

No published work applies process mining to building HVAC fault detection. The 211-study review has no process-mining entry in its taxonomy of 23 methods \cite{bi2024aihvacreview}, and the LBNL taxonomy of fault-detection methods does not list it \cite{granderson2017fddtoolsurvey}. From the other side, a review of 47 process-mining studies in Industry 4.0 \cite{akhramovich2024pmindustry40}, a review of 36 on sensor data covering industry, smart homes and healthcare \cite{brzychczy2025pmsensorreview}, and a review of 93 on process management with the Internet of Things \cite{deluzi2025bpmiotreview} contain no building application. A title-and-abstract search of the OpenAlex index for process mining together with HVAC, air handling, building automation, building energy or chillers, run on 16 October 2026, returns no work since 2023, and the programmes of the process-mining conferences and workshops of 2024 to 2026 contain none. The nearest work analyses building-management alarm logs statistically, without discovering a process model from them \cite{shahid2026alarmlogs}.

Katipamula and Brambley divide the problem into detection, isolation and identification: noticing that a fault exists, locating it, and naming its cause \cite{katipamula2005fddreview1}. The rule libraries and learned detectors above stop at detection; only the two 2026 studies of Park and Paolini reach isolation. STRATA does detection and isolation, and sits between the two established groups. It learns from fault-free data what the building's normal operation is, which a rule library cannot, and it reports a location, the evidence and a measured false-alarm rate, which a score cannot. It is a complement to a rule library rather than a replacement, and its located alarms are the kind of input the LBNL correction algorithms were built to act on. Identification of the cause, and correction, are the later stages of ProcessHeal, the self-healing building toward which STRATA is the first step.

\subsection{Stage 1: the building's data and one configuration file}

The input is the trend data a building-automation system already records: temperatures, flows, pressures, damper and valve positions, fan speeds, usually once a minute. STRATA does not need new sensors. It needs one fault-free period of that data, ideally a year so that every season is seen, and one configuration file.

The configuration file is the only thing that is specific to a building. It has two parts. The first maps the building's sensor names to the roles the framework understands: for example, which column holds the supply-air temperature and which holds each zone's damper position. The second lists the control sequence for this type of equipment as rules, taken from the design documents, from the ASHRAE Guideline 36 sequences \cite{ashrae2021guideline36} and, as Stage 3 describes, from invariants found in the fault-free data. These rules come in two families. State rules say when the unit is occupied and when it is in heating or cooling mode. Signature rules say which relationships must hold, such as no flow through a closed valve.

Onboarding a new building means writing this file; the framework's code does not change. On the benchmarks, once the framework existed, configuring a second equipment type took about one and a half hours. Two of the five main benchmark systems were configured before any fault data was opened, so nothing in them could have been tuned to the faults.

\subsection{Stage 2: turning sensor data into events}

Process mining works on event logs, not on sensor curves, so the first thing the framework does with the data is turn the minute-by-minute values into events along the control sequence. Each day is one case. An event is, for example, ``fan started at 06:02'' or ``cooling became active at 09:14''. The configuration's rules produce them: a state rule for operating mode fires an event when the mode changes, and a signature rule fires one when the relationship it guards is broken.

There are two kinds of events, and keeping them apart is a central design decision. \emph{State events} describe what the equipment is doing, without any judgement of whether it is correct. \emph{Signature events} describe something that should not happen, such as ``water flowing through a valve that is commanded closed''. Only the state events are allowed into the next stage, model discovery. If the signature events were allowed in, the discovered model would simply learn the configured fault rules and later ``detect'' them, which would be circular. We measured the effect: on one system, letting signature events into discovery raised the unit conformance check's apparent detection from 1.8 to 5.2 percent of fault days, and every added day was the model re-finding a rule we had written.

\subsection{Stage 3: discovering what normal looks like}

From the state-event log of the fault-free period, the framework discovers process models of a normal day with a standard process-mining algorithm, the inductive miner \cite{leemans2013inductiveminer}. It does this at two levels; the levels are the strata the name refers to. The \emph{terminal-unit} level has one model per terminal unit, describing the normal day of that terminal unit's damper and reheat valve. The \emph{unit} level has one model for the unit's whole daily cycle (the air handler, in the systems that have terminal units). Each model is a graph of the states the terminal unit or unit passes through and the order it passes through them (a Petri net, in process-mining terms).

This structure does two things in the framework. First, it gives every alarm its location: events, rules and models are all defined per terminal unit, so any check that fires on one terminal unit's data names that terminal unit, and a check that fires on the air handler's data names the air handler. Second, during development the models surfaced invariants of normal operation, things that were always true on fault-free days, which we then wrote down as rules in the configuration. The models do not detect faults by themselves; Section~\ref{sec:pm} reports the test that established this.

\subsection{Stage 4: calibration, then eight checks}

\emph{Calibration (4a).} Every threshold in every check comes from the fault-free period only. The year is split by calendar date: the last eight days of each month (96 days in a year) are held out and never used for setting anything; they exist only to measure how often each check gives a false alarm. The remaining days, the training days, set the healthy bands and train the models. No threshold ever sees fault data, and no threshold sees the days on which its false-alarm rate is later reported.

\emph{The checks (4b).} Each day is scored by eight checks, each of which alarms on its own and is named in the alarm it raises. Three of them read the raw sensor data:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Rules}: a control-sequence rule was broken, such as flow through a closed valve, or a zone's airflow far from its setpoint while occupied.
\item \emph{Residual}: a physical relationship that must hold in a known mode has drifted out of its healthy band. With the cooling coil off, the supply-air temperature should equal the mixed-air temperature plus the small rise from the fan; if it does not, a sensor is biased or a coil is leaking.
\item \emph{Oscillation}: an actuator reversed direction far more often than it does on fault-free days, the sign of an unstable control loop.
\end{itemize}
Five of them read the event log:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Unit conformance} and \emph{terminal-unit conformance}: how well today's sequence of events fits the discovered model of the unit or of the terminal unit. A day fits poorly when, for example, cooling starts before the fan, an order the fault-free period never showed.
\item \emph{Absence}: a terminal unit that produces events on every fault-free occupied day produced none on an occupied day.
\item \emph{Frequency}: a daily count, such as the number of heating episodes, left its healthy band.
\item \emph{Rate}: the number of the last 30 days on which a mode was active, compared with the same calendar month of the fault-free year. It never raises an alarm alone, because on one system it added false alarms; it is reported beside another check's alarm as corroborating evidence.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Stage 5: the noise gate}

A check is allowed to raise an alarm on a scenario only when the number of days it flagged is too large to be explained by its own false-alarm rate. The test is a binomial test, the same calculation used for coin tosses. Suppose a check wrongly flags three of 96 held-out fault-free days. Flagging forty of 365 days in a scenario is then far beyond what chance would give, and the check may alarm; flagging five is within chance, and the check may not alarm. For the test, the rate is never taken below three days in 96, so a check with no false alarms on the held-out days is not treated as perfect. The alarm itself reports the measured count, which can be zero, as in Figure~\ref{fig:alarm}.

On the benchmarks the test is applied once per check per scenario, to decide whether the check is credited with the detection; the first day the credited check flagged is the day of the alarm. On a live building there is no scenario, only days. How the test is applied there, for example over a rolling window of recent days, is a decision for the planned trial (step 4 of Table~\ref{tab:deploy}). What is already fixed is that every check's false-alarm rate is measured on the held-out days before the first alarm and is reported with each alarm, so the operator knows how often that check raises a false one.

\subsection{Stage 6: the alarm the operator receives}

What survives the gate reaches the operator with four things: what broke, where, the evidence, and the check's measured false-alarm rate. Figure~\ref{fig:alarm} shows one, assembled from the framework's recorded output. The fault seeded was a zone damper stuck at 80 percent open. The alarm names the zone, names the terminal unit, states that the primary airflow is 515 cubic feet per minute (CFM) above its setpoint and has been for twelve hours while occupied, reports the damper position of 0.80 against a normal 0.32, and tells the operator that the rules check gave zero false alarms on the 96 held-out fault-free days. It was raised on the first day of the fault.

\section{Results on Public Benchmark Data}
\label{sec:results}

We evaluated the framework on the public HVAC fault-detection datasets from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory \cite{granderson2023lbnlfdddata}. Each simulated dataset is one-minute data for one type of equipment, recorded once fault-free and once per seeded fault: a stuck damper, a biased sensor, a fouled coil, a leaking valve, an unstable controller. The five main systems have a full year of data.

Table~\ref{tab:systems} lists the seven datasets: air handlers that serve many zones through one duct (single-duct) or through separate hot and cold ducts (dual-duct), two kinds of fan-powered terminal unit, a fan coil unit that serves one room, and two rooftop units. Six are simulated; one is a measured year from a real rooftop unit. We found no published detection results on the fan-powered or dual-duct datasets. Unless stated, the results below are for the five main systems. The sixth dataset, a simulated rooftop unit, is the only one that records refrigerant-side measurements, so it was used for the faults that are visible only on that side. The measured unit has 182 fault-free days and one fault case, so it yields a false-alarm rate but no detection count.

\subsection{Detection and false alarms}

Across the five main systems the framework detected 158 of 175 seeded faults, with 26 false-alarm days in 480 held-out fault-free days, between 1 and 9.4 percent per system. The median time from the start of a fault to its first alarm was one day. On the real rooftop unit the false-alarm rate was 3 of 49 held-out days. A later audit of the configurations mapped every recorded column that the first configurations had left unread; sixteen of the 158 detections came from columns that audit added. The 17 faults it missed are all coil fouling. For some coils every seeded severity was missed, because the recorded sensor values stay inside every healthy band; nothing that reads only those values could see them. On the one dataset that records the refrigerant side, the framework caught every fouling severity.

\subsection{Where the alarm points}

On the two fan-powered systems, where each air handler serves four terminal units, 50 detections made before the column audit had their seeded fault in one terminal unit. In 37 of these the alarm named the correct terminal unit, in 13 it named the air handler but no terminal unit, and in none did it name a wrong one. Over all 158 detections the rule carrying the alarm pointed at the right class of component (coil, fan, damper or terminal unit) in 83 percent of cases and at the exact seeded component in 69 percent. The alarm names the symptom and the place. It does not name the root cause: a first diagnosis layer we tested improved localisation slightly but did not reach the targets we had set for it, so we make no diagnosis claim.

\subsection{Against two baselines}

Against principal component analysis, a standard statistical method applied to the raw sensors, the framework detected 64 of 73 shared scenarios against the baseline's 61, a difference too small to be statistically significant. The baseline cannot name the terminal unit. Against the Guideline 36 fault rules that commercial systems run, implemented by an independent open-source library and scored under the same gate, the framework detected one more fault than the rules on the single-duct air handler at the same false-alarm count, and three times as many on the terminal units, because it reads the per-zone sensors the rules do not (Table~\ref{tab:g36}). We had predicted that STRATA would detect at least as many faults as the rules at a lower false-alarm rate on every system. It did not: on the air handler the false-alarm counts were equal, and on the terminal units the extra coverage cost eight and nine false-alarm days against the rules' none.

\subsection{What process mining contributes}
\label{sec:pm}

We began with the hypothesis that the discovered process models would detect the faults themselves. They did not. Over 199 scenarios on six systems (the 175 plus the 24 on the simulated rooftop unit), no fault was caught by the conformance checks alone, and even on the scenario where the models did best, a one-line hand-written rule flagged more fault days than the model did. What process mining contributes is structure. The per-terminal-unit models give every alarm its location. The models surfaced invariants that became detection rules, for example that the air handler's heating never runs on an occupied workday. On the two fan-powered systems the frequency check, which counts events in the log, is one of only two checks (with the oscillation check) that see the unstable-controller faults. Process mining helps to get detection but is not detecting on its own. That finding came from pre-registered tests, with the refuting result written down before each one was run, and it is why detection rests on the calibrated checks while the models supply structure and location.

\subsection{Robustness to noisier data}

Simulated data is cleaner than a real building's. To check that this had not inflated the results, we applied four perturbations to the fault-free year and every fault file: sensor noise; change-of-value logging, which records a value only when it changes, as many real systems do; gaps in the record; and shifted schedules. We then re-fitted and re-scored all five systems. Before running this test we had set a limit of 10 false-alarm days in 96. Every system stayed under it, with the worst count at 7 of 96, and each condition cost at most one detection per system.

Three things decide whether it works on a real building, and all three are checked before the first alarm is trusted. First, the fault-free period must really be fault-free: one faulty day in it can move a band edge, so the period is audited first. Second, the configuration must read every recorded point; the column audit of Section~\ref{sec:results} showed what unread columns cost. Third, the silence step must run at the building's own sampling rate, because thresholds set on one-minute data do not transfer to fifteen-minute data.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

Every detection result above comes from simulated data, and simulated years are cleaner than real ones; the one real building so far supports a false-alarm rate, not a detection rate. Two scoring choices were made after we saw their effect. The rate check was demoted to support-only, and the two conformance checks, whose thresholds had been set on the same days later used to measure their false-alarm rates, were re-set on separate days, which raised the unit conformance false-alarm rates on the two fan-powered systems. Slowly developing faults are caught late, up to 114 days for two fouling cases. The benchmarks seed equipment and sensor faults, not the schedule and override faults that are most common in real buildings, such as a unit left in manual or a schedule left in holiday mode. Finally, the alarm names the symptom and the location, not the cause; diagnosis and automated correction are the later stages of ProcessHeal, and would need active tests and an operator study before any automated action.

\section{Conclusion}

STRATA turns the trend data a building already records into alarms an operator can act on: it learns normal operation from one fault-free period, checks each day against physics and against discovered process models, lets through only alarms that beat a measured false-alarm rate, and states what broke, where, the evidence and that rate. On the five main benchmark systems it detected 158 of 175 seeded faults, with the false-alarm rate of every check measured on held-out days and reported with each alarm, and, in the 50 cases where this could be checked, never named a wrong terminal unit. It is onboarded with a configuration file rather than new code. Process mining gives the framework its structure and the location on every alarm; the detecting is done by the calibrated checks. The next step is a live trial on a real building, and the planned site is the TRU campus.
